Free aerial photos and an open AI model can measure how full a Lille district's public parking is, and the answer changes a lot from one date to the next

We counted the cars parked on the streets and public car parks of Wazemmes (about 1 km²) on three IGN photos, turned the count into an occupancy rate using the parking spaces mapped in OpenStreetMap, and corrected it for the model's measured errors and for cars hidden under trees.

How it works, step by step

  1. Enlarge and cut the photo. The photo is resized to 10 cm per pixel, the scale the model learned on, and cut into 64 m squares that overlap so no car is cut in half.
  2. Find the cars. An object-detection model (YOLO11n, trained by us on over 32,000 cars in the COWC aerial photo collection) gives every car-like shape a confidence score. We keep marks scoring 0.10 or more: that cut-off found the most cars for the fewest false marks when tested on squares the model had not seen. Two marks closer than 1.5 m become one.
  3. Keep only cars where parking is allowed. Marks outside the streets and car parks are thrown away. That removes most false marks, which sit on roofs.
  4. Count the spaces. Along a kerb: its length divided by 5.5 m for cars parked in a line, 3.0 m when angled, 2.5 m when side by side, minus room for crossings. A car park: its stated capacity, or one space per 25 m² including aisles. On Wazemmes these lines and outlines come from OpenStreetMap.
  5. Correct the count. The model misses more cars than it invents, so the count is multiplied by precision ÷ recall (0.895 ÷ 0.819 on the Wazemmes streets). Cars under trees cannot be seen, so the count is divided by the share of parking that is not under trees (from the infrared photo on Wazemmes, your estimate on your photo).
  6. Free spaces = spaces − corrected cars, with a range from the best and worst error rates we measured on three dates (correction factor from 1.06 to 1.22).

What it cannot do: tell a parked car from one waiting at a light, see cars in underground or multi-storey car parks, or know which spaces are reserved (deliveries, disabled drivers, residents). It counts what one photo shows at one moment.

City streets are much harder than the retail car parks of our first prototype

Our first prototype counted cars in the Auchan Englos car parks. The same model, with the same cut-off (0.25) and the same scoring rule, finds 91% of the cars there but only 64% on the Wazemmes streets. The two squares below show why: blue rings are cars found, red rings cars missed, orange rings false marks.

Shadows and leaning buildings

Four- and five-storey buildings throw their shadow across one side of the street, and in an aerial photo they lean over the kerb. Cars in that strip are dark on dark, or partly hidden: many of the red rings sit there.

Cars nose to tail, under trees

On a street, cars are parked bumper to bumper along the kerb with no painted bays between them, so neighbours merge. Street trees cover 11% of the district and up to 44% of some streets.

Roofs full of car-sized things

Skylights, chimneys, vents and small sheds are the size of a car from 20 cm per pixel. A car park is open asphalt; a city block is roofs, so false marks pile up there. Keeping only marks on mapped streets and car parks lifts the real-car share from 65% to 90% at the 0.10 cut-off we use.

Why this matters: a count from a retail car park would overstate how well the method works in a city. That is why we measured the errors on the streets themselves and correct every estimate for them.
