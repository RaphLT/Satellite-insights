## B. What works, what does not, what it was tested on, who did what

**Prototype 2 (question A).** The car counter runs in Colab or on a laptop with free tools only, and nothing is trained. On COWC Potsdam tile 2_12 (113 cars marked by hand), it found 92 cars with 6 false alarms (81% found, 94% right). On the IGN photo of Auchan Englos it counted 2,217 cars. On one area of that photo, checked by eye (~103 cars), it found 89 with 1 false alarm and missed 14, mostly dark cars in shadow. What does not work: the settings do not transfer between image sources. Sharpening helps IGN and hurts COWC, and without ×5 zoom the model finds almost nothing on IGN. It was tested on one labelled tile only, and the IGN image is a ~25 cm copy of unknown date. The count covers the whole retail park, roads included, not Auchan's customers alone.

**Prototype 3 (train our own model).** Works: a small model that had never seen an aerial image learned to find cars from 515 hand-labelled examples in about an hour on a CPU. On the unseen COWC tile 2_12 it found 65% of cars, and 66% of its detections were real cars. The ready-made model, trained on DOTA's 188,282 labelled objects, scored 81% and 94% on the same tile. On the Englos hand-check area both models found about 87–89 of ~100 cars. Does not work well: our model raises false alarms on roof windows and grass. It was tested on one tile (113 cars). It also differs from the ready-made model in size (small vs medium), not only in training data.

**Prototype 4 (Englos over time, in progress).** Counts from 2004 and 2009 are not usable: at 50 cm per pixel, the detector finds 0 and 24 cars where many are visible by eye. The 2015–2025 counts are still to be run. IGN does not say which of several days each photo was taken on, and one candidate (8 May 2018) is a public holiday, so year-to-year changes cannot be read as changes in Auchan's business.

**Prototype 1 (signal decay).**


The prototype runs in any browser without a login and in Google Colab. For each set of assumptions, it computes the number of client funds that maximises a data vendor's revenue, the price that number implies, and the revenue at the price the article reports. The web page and the notebook give identical results. The notebook checks its own arithmetic: the exclusive-fund profit equals 4.76% × capital × 4, and under the "fixed pie" curve total profit stays the same for any number of funds. It does **not** use satellite images or any Orbital Insight sales data. It is a toy model whose central input, how fast a fund's edge shrinks as more funds buy the signal, is unknown, so we show four curves instead of one answer. Its revenues are upper bounds, because we assume the vendor captures all of each fund's profit. It also covers only the retail car-count product. We tested it on real inputs from Katona et al. (2025): the 4.76% three-day return in Table 3 and the "hundreds of thousands" price on p. 556. We also used Orbital Insight's $130M of funding and $9.5M of expected 2023 revenue (Poulson, 2023). Capital per fund, processing cost, the exact price and the value of a non-rival client are our assumptions, and the sliders let readers change them.
