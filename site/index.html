Was selling AI-processed satellite data to investors a viable business?

The case is Orbital Insight (Palo Alto, founded 2013, sold to Privateer in May 2024). It counted cars in retailers' parking lots from satellite images and sold the counts to hedge funds, so they could guess a retailer's sales before the official figures came out. Our main source is Katona, Painter, Patatoukas & Zeng (2025), On the capital market consequences of big data: Evidence from outer space, JFQA 60(2).

Our answer in two sentences. The car-count signal was worth most while few funds had it, and it lost value as it spread, so a business built on selling exclusive data could not carry the ~$130M of venture capital Orbital Insight raised (its expected 2023 revenue was ~$9.5M). A new entrant needs customers who do not need exclusivity, because the AI itself is now close to free.

Four prototypes, one story

Each prototype tests one step of the argument with real data from our case. They were built by "vibe-coding": an AI assistant wrote the code, we directed it and checked the results. Every prompt is in the appendix.

1 · The business

How a data signal loses value

An interactive toy market: one vendor sells the same signal to more and more funds. How many clients, at what price?

At a price in the range the article reports, three of four curves imply 7–13 clients and $2–6M of yearly revenue, far below what $130M of funding needs.
2 · The technology

Can a student rebuild the car counter?

A free, ready-made detector, tested on cars marked by hand and on a free photo of the Auchan Englos retail park near Lille.

It found 81% of hand-marked cars, and 94% of what it counted were real cars. 2,217 cars counted at Englos.
3 · The data behind the AI

Training our own model

We trained a small detector ourselves on 515 hand-marked cars, in about an hour on a laptop processor.

It found 65% of cars (ready-made: 81%). The algorithm is free; the labelled examples make the difference.
